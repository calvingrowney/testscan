# Premium Tuna Brand Investigation: project roadmap

## The question

**Could a direct-to-consumer, bulk-purchase brand of premium tuna earn a place in the routines of fitness-minded college students and 20-somethings—and create a credible platform for future high-protein foods?**

The point is not to make a pretty tuna label. The point is to investigate a real consumer and business question with evidence, make defensible creative decisions, test them, and document the reasoning.

## Core hypothesis

Active young adults may value a convenient, shelf-stable protein option, but tuna can carry barriers: perceived smell, boredom, price sensitivity, mercury/sourcing concerns, limited kitchen access, and distrust of marketing that feels overly "gym bro." The project earns a brand only if it finds a specific, sizeable-enough tension the concept can credibly solve.

## Non-negotiable guardrails

- This is a **student concept and research project**, not food-business or legal advice.
- Never present an invented product, sourcing claim, nutrition value, test result, partnership, or review as real.
- A "protein-rich," performance, health, low-mercury, sustainable, or comparative claim stays off packaging and marketing until it is verified for the exact finished product by qualified professionals.
- Do not collect sensitive personal data. For interviews and surveys, ask for consent, keep raw notes private, and report results in aggregate.
- Makenna owns every decision. Capture both the AI recommendation and her response, especially when she rejects it.

## Suggested rhythm

Plan on 8–12 focused sessions (roughly one phase per week). Move faster only when a gate has sufficient evidence—not just enthusiasm. Each phase ends with: (1) a saved artifact, (2) an evidence summary, (3) a decision-log entry, and (4) a parent/Makenna gate.

## Phases and gates

### 0. Set up the investigation

**Question:** What are we trying to learn, and what would change our minds?

**Work**

1. Read this roadmap and create `evidence/` and `deliverables/`.
2. Write a one-page learning brief: question, assumptions, target audience, constraints, what success looks like, and what would cause a no-go.
3. Create an assumptions table. Give every assumption an owner, evidence needed, and a status: untested / supported / disproven.
4. Start the [decision log](../templates/decision-log.md).

**Agents:** Project Steward, Research Agent, Skeptic Agent.

**Artifacts:** `deliverables/00-learning-brief.md`, `evidence/00-assumptions.md`.

**Gate 0 — research brief approved**

Makenna and parent approve the central question, safe scope, target audience, and no-go criteria. No visual identity work yet.

---

### 1. Map the market and the white-label reality

**Question:** Is there a plausible space between commodity tuna and existing premium/protein brands?

**Work**

1. Build a competitor map of 20–30 brands: tuna/seafood, meal-prep protein, snack protein, and premium pantry brands. Record product format, species, flavor, pack size, price, shipping/subscription model, ingredients, nutrition panel, claims, visual cues, target audience, and proof behind key claims.
2. Separate direct competitors from substitutes: chicken pouches, jerky, protein bars, canned salmon, ready-to-eat meals, etc.
3. Research possible routes: existing U.S. co-packer/private-label supplier, distributor resale, or importing. Do **not** imply a supplier relationship without written confirmation.
4. Create a preliminary unit-economics range: product, pouch/can, freight, fulfillment, payment fees, returns, customer acquisition, and gross-margin assumptions. Use ranges and cite inputs; never use a single made-up margin.
5. List operational and regulatory questions to take to a real food-business advisor/co-packer if the concept survives.

**Agents:** Market Research Agent, Operations/Sourcing Agent, Finance Reality-Check Agent, Skeptic Agent.

**Artifacts:** competitor matrix, supplier-question list, unit-economics model, "why now / why not" memo.

**Gate 1 — opportunity worth interviewing**

Advance only if Makenna can state a specific opening, the strongest existing alternatives, and at least three serious risks. A valid outcome is **no-go** or a pivot (for example, a different seafood species or a broader convenience-protein concept).

---

### 2. Talk to real people before choosing a solution

**Question:** Do intended customers experience a meaningful, frequent problem that tuna could solve?

**Work**

1. Use the [interview guide](../templates/interview-guide.md) to speak with 12–15 people in the intended audience. Aim for a mix of college students, recent graduates, regular gym-goers, and non-gym-goers who buy convenient protein.
2. Ask about actual last-week behavior—not whether they "would buy" the product. Prompt for routines, budget, kitchen access, what they already buy, and tradeoffs.
3. Run a small anonymous survey only after interviews reveal good language and hypotheses. Include price and pack-size tradeoffs, but avoid leading questions.
4. Code findings into themes: jobs to be done, obstacles, desired outcomes, language, and segments. Keep raw notes private and de-identify quotes.
5. Put agent predictions beside the actual evidence. Record surprises and contradictions.

**Agents:** Interview Coach, Insight Synthesizer, Skeptic Agent.

**Artifacts:** consent note, anonymized interview notes, survey, findings deck/memo, "where AI was wrong" page.

**Gate 2 — target segment and problem approved**

Choose one initial segment and one core problem only if at least several independent interviews support it. Otherwise revise the target or return to Gate 1. Do not choose a name or logo yet.

---

### 3. Form and test the strategy

**Question:** What could this brand credibly promise that customers care about and alternatives do not own?

**Work**

1. Write 3–4 positioning territories, each built from evidence: customer, tension, category frame, promise, reasons to believe, personality, and exclusions.
2. Have the Skeptic Agent try to destroy each territory: sameness, audience mismatch, credibility gaps, price resistance, brand-extension risk, and cultural awkwardness.
3. Turn the strongest 2–3 territories into plain-language concept cards. Keep visual design intentionally minimal.
4. Test concept cards with 15–25 prospective customers. Randomize order, ask for reactions and tradeoffs, and include a real alternative. Measure comprehension and preference but treat the sample as directional.
5. Write the strategic choice and the alternatives deliberately rejected.

**Agents:** Strategy Agent, Skeptic Agent, Concept-Test Analyst.

**Artifacts:** positioning territory cards, critique memo, concept-test results, approved brand platform.

**Gate 3 — positioning approved**

Makenna approves exactly one positioning direction. It must be understandable without a logo and must avoid unsubstantiated health/performance claims. If no territory wins, revise the problem or choose no-go.

---

### 4. Prove the product and claims constraints

**Question:** Can a particular product, format, and sourcing story support the desired experience and words?

**Work**

1. Specify a hypothetical MVP: species, form (pouch/can/kit), net weight, flavors, serving count, bundle size, target price range, shelf-life expectation, and source-country/processor questions.
2. Build a **claim register** using the template. Every word customers could interpret as a factual promise goes in the register: protein, mercury, omega-3, clean, sustainably caught, wild, traceable, athlete/performance, etc.
3. For every claim, document the exact evidence needed and who can validate it (labeling specialist, accredited lab, supplier documentation, certification body, attorney). Until validated, replace it with neutral placeholder language or remove it.
4. Review seafood safety, product identity, labeling, allergen disclosure, import/co-packer, and shipping questions with qualified professionals if moving beyond a concept. Research does not substitute for professional approval.
5. Decide whether the economics and operational route are plausible enough for a concept launch.

**Agents:** Claims Auditor, Sourcing Agent, Finance Reality-Check Agent. Agents may identify questions; they do not approve a label.

**Artifacts:** MVP product brief, claim register, risk register, go/no-go memo.

**Gate 4 — product direction and claims boundaries approved**

Makenna and parent approve a clearly marked *concept-only* product direction. If a real launch is contemplated, pause for legal, food-safety, labeling, supply-chain, insurance, and financial professional review before any sale, sample distribution, or marketing claim.

---

### 5. Create the brand identity

**Question:** What identity makes the strategy immediately recognizable without looking like generic fitness food?

**Work**

1. Translate the approved strategy into a creative brief: audience, job, promise, proof boundaries, personality, taboo cues, visual principles, and design requirements.
2. Research visual codes across adjacent categories. Distinguish useful category recognition from copying.
3. Develop 3 identity territories—not isolated logos. Each includes wordmark direction, color/typography logic, imagery/art direction, packaging front, social tile, and a written rationale.
4. Test territories for clarity, distinctiveness, appropriateness, and shelf/screen legibility with 10–15 intended customers.
5. Run an originality and trademark-risk screen. Do not treat an agent search as clearance; obtain professional trademark review before use in commerce.

**Agents:** Creative Director Assistant, Visual-Research Agent, Brand QA Agent, Skeptic Agent.

**Artifacts:** creative brief, moodboards with source attribution, three identity systems, feedback report, chosen identity specification.

**Gate 5 — identity approved**

Makenna chooses or revises one territory only after it is tested against strategy, audience feedback, and basic originality concerns. Parent approves the final logo/identity direction. Keep rejected directions in the case-study archive.

---

### 6. Build product, packaging, and conversion prototypes

**Question:** Can the approved strategy work in the moments of purchase, storage, eating, and reordering?

**Work**

1. Design concept packaging and product photography/mockups. Clearly label mockups as conceptual; use licensed or self-created assets.
2. Create an unregulated prototype label layout with placeholder facts only. Do not print, distribute, or market it as a compliant product label.
3. Design DTC bundle architecture: starter box, recurring bundle, variety pack, and a simple comparison against alternatives.
4. Create a landing page prototype, product detail page, subscription/bundle selection, FAQ, and post-purchase flow. Make product status clear.
5. Conduct 5 usability sessions: Can people explain the offer, protein amount/serving (if verified), price, shipping, and why it differs? Record confusion.

**Agents:** UX Agent, Packaging Critic, Conversion Copy Agent, Brand QA Agent.

**Artifacts:** packaging concepts, website prototype, usability notes, revised copy, bundle map.

**Gate 6 — packaging and website prototype approved**

Approve a concept presentation only after it is legible, consistent, and makes no unsupported or misleading claims. A real label/site needs professional compliance review before public commerce.

---

### 7. Plan the launch experiment and extensions

**Question:** What would responsibly test interest before committing inventory, and can the brand extend beyond tuna?

**Work**

1. Choose a low-risk concept test: landing-page comprehension test, unbranded preference test, waitlist simulation clearly labeled as concept research, or interviews with retail/food-service buyers. Do not take orders for an unavailable food product.
2. Define success metrics *before* the test: comprehension, email-interest rate only if consented, preferred format, acceptable price range, and objections. Avoid treating likes as demand.
3. Create a 30-day hypothetical marketing plan: creator criteria, content pillars, email/SMS flows, campus/community partnerships, paid-media experiments, budget guardrails, and measurement plan.
4. Evaluate future categories using a brand-extension scorecard: same customer, same occasion, same proof system, supply-chain plausibility, and risk of dilution.

**Agents:** Growth Strategy Agent, Experiment Designer, Brand-Extension Skeptic.

**Artifacts:** research-test plan, marketing plan, extension scorecard, results memo.

**Gate 7 — public concept test approved**

Parent approves any public-facing test, its disclosures, privacy approach, budget, and success criteria. Makenna decides what she learned and what she would do next; success is learning, not merely a high response count.

---

### 8. Publish the learning, not just the outcome

**Question:** How can this show Makenna's curiosity, judgment, and ability to direct agents?

**Work**

1. Build the case study using the portfolio outline.
2. Include evidence, decisions, iterations, rejected ideas, the agent system, and "Where AI was wrong."
3. Separate fictional concepts from validated facts and avoid exposing private interviewee data.
4. Have an outside reader answer: What question did she investigate? What did she personally decide? What evidence changed her mind?

**Agents:** Portfolio Editor, Evidence Checker, Brand QA Agent.

**Artifacts:** case-study page, downloadable process book/PDF, selected deliverable gallery, reflection.

**Gate 8 — portfolio publication approved**

Makenna and parent verify privacy, attribution, disclosures, and accuracy. Domain and website platform remain open decisions until the case study is ready.

## Decision authority

| Decision | Makenna | Parent | Qualified professional, if moving beyond a concept |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Research question, interviews, brand strategy, visual direction | final decision | approve scope/safety | — |
| Public testing, spending, contact with suppliers/creators | recommends | final approval | — |
| Food safety, label compliance, health/nutrition/sustainability claims, trademark clearance, selling food | contributes research | approves engagement | final approval |

## Completion definition

The project is complete when it has a defensible answer—not necessarily a positive one—to the core question; an approved concept identity and DTC prototype; a testable marketing strategy; a transparent claims/risk record; and a public-ready case study showing Makenna's own thinking.
